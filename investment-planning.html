Investment Planning

Why investment
decisions should
start with the goal.

Before asking where to invest, it helps to understand what the money is there to achieve.

Purpose first.
Portfolio second.

Start somewhere different

Don't start with the investment.
Start with the reason for it.

Investment planning is not simply about choosing funds, watching markets or trying to find the investment that performed best last year.

Investments are financial tools. What matters is what you need those tools to do.

That means understanding your goals, timescale, circumstances and attitude to risk before deciding how your money should be invested.

Goal first.
Money second.

Give the money a job

What is this investment
actually for?

Different goals can require very different approaches.

Money you expect to need relatively soon has a different job from money intended to support retirement many years from now.

Investments intended to provide future income may need to be considered differently from money being accumulated for longer-term growth.

And money intended eventually to support children or other family members may have a completely different timescale again.

Until the purpose is clear, it is difficult to decide what an appropriate investment strategy should look like.

Start with the questions

What does the money
need to achieve?

A sensible investment conversation begins with your life and financial objectives.

What is the money for? When might you need it? Will you need income from it? How much investment risk is appropriate? What other assets do you already have? How does it fit your wider plan?

Investment structures

The investment and the
wrapper aren't the same thing.

UK investors may hold investments through structures such as Individual Savings Accounts — ISAs — General Investment Accounts and Self-Invested Personal Pensions, or SIPPs.

The appropriate structure depends on the individual, the purpose of the money and the wider financial plan.

Inside those structures sit the actual investments.

Understanding that distinction can make the overall picture much clearer.

Investment risk

Risk isn't just a
questionnaire score.

Investment values can rise and fall, and different investments expose you to different types and levels of risk.

Understanding how comfortable you are with investment risk matters, but it is only part of the picture.

Your financial ability to withstand losses, the amount of time available and the importance of the goal also need to be considered.

A level of risk that may be reasonable for one objective may be completely inappropriate for another.

Remember

Investment values
don't move in straight lines.

Markets rise and fall. A long-term investment strategy needs to recognise that uncertainty rather than pretending it can be removed.

Stay focused
on the plan.

Building a portfolio

Don't make one investment
carry the whole future.

Diversification is about spreading investment exposure rather than relying too heavily on one company, market, sector or type of asset.

A portfolio can contain different investments designed to work together rather than expecting one holding to deliver everything.

The appropriate balance depends on the investor, their goals and the amount of risk suitable for their circumstances.

Invest for the plan.
Not for the noise.

Costs matter

Performance isn't the
only number worth watching.

Investment and product charges can reduce the returns that remain available to you.

That doesn't mean the cheapest option is automatically the most appropriate.

It does mean you should understand what you are paying, what you are receiving in return and whether those costs remain reasonable.

This becomes particularly important with older investment arrangements that may have accumulated layers of product, fund and adviser charges.

Investment approach

Active, passive — or
a combination?

Some investment funds are actively managed, with a fund manager making decisions about what to buy and sell.

Others use a passive approach designed to track a particular market or index.

Both approaches can have a role.

The important question is not which label sounds better. It is whether the investment is suitable for the role it is expected to play within the portfolio, and whether its costs and characteristics are understood.

Keep reviewing

A portfolio shouldn't
be built and forgotten.

Over time, investment performance and changes in your own life can alter the balance of a portfolio.

Your goals may change Your timescale may change Your attitude to risk may change Investments may perform differently Costs may need reviewing The portfolio may need rebalancing

If some investments grow faster than others, the portfolio can gradually move away from its intended balance.

Regular reviews provide an opportunity to check whether the strategy still reflects your goals, circumstances and attitude to risk.

Where appropriate, that may mean rebalancing the portfolio or reconsidering individual holdings.

The purpose isn't to make changes for the sake of activity. It is to keep the investments aligned with the plan they were built to support.

Ignore some of the noise

The best-performing investment
changes all the time.

Markets generate an enormous amount of noise.

There will always be another headline, another prediction and another investment that has just produced spectacular returns.

Chasing yesterday's winner can distract from the reason you invested in the first place.

A structured financial plan gives investment decisions context and provides something more useful to focus on than the latest headline.

The bigger picture

Investing isn't the goal.
Your goal is the goal.

A portfolio should have a purpose within your wider financial plan.

Before asking “What should I invest in?” it may be more useful to ask “What am I investing for?”

The value of investments can fall as well as rise and you may get back less than you invest. The suitability of any investment or investment structure depends on individual circumstances, objectives and attitude to risk.
