Portfolio review in practice

Sometimes the problem isn't
what you're doing now.

One of the aspects of my job that gives me the most satisfaction is helping clients get out of unsuitable products or portfolios they acquired through previous advisers.

Many older pension and investment products carry high ongoing charges and may still be invested in expensive active funds that have not been properly reviewed for years.

Old plan.
Fresh eyes.

The starting point

Meet Peter.

I met a new client called Peter around three years before originally writing this case study. At that stage I could only take over the ongoing servicing of his existing plan.

As it turned out, that gave me time to plan a successful exit for him.

Peter had invested around £720,000 into a Self-Invested Personal Pension — a SIPP.

Within the SIPP was an investment bond from a life assurance company, which had been used to hold a portfolio of funds recommended by his previous adviser.

The problem was that the portfolio had not been reviewed since Peter had met that adviser around six years earlier.

In fact, Peter had not seen or heard from the adviser for several years. When he eventually tried the mobile number on the business card he had kept, the number was unobtainable.

His accountant subsequently introduced him to me, believing that he needed some professional financial planning.

What had happened?

Heavy withdrawals.
Volatile markets.

Peter had been drawing heavily from his pension fund during a particularly volatile period for world stock markets.

He had withdrawn £280,000, including his 25% tax-free cash sum, reducing the value of the fund to approximately £490,000.

Once the cash withdrawal was taken into account, he had made only around £50,000 over the preceding six years — just under 10% of the £540,000 that had remained invested.

The complication

Getting out wasn't
straightforward.

The investment bond still carried surrender penalties, reducing on a sliding scale during its first ten years.

Sometimes
timing matters.

Peter was also paying an ongoing product charge of 1% per year for the lifetime of the product.

At that point, paying the surrender penalty to leave immediately was difficult to justify.

The structure had been designed in part to recoup the substantial commission paid to the original adviser if the bond was surrendered during its first ten years.

So rather than immediately dismantling the entire arrangement, I initially concentrated on what could be changed: the underlying investment portfolio.

Looking underneath

The portfolio itself
needed attention.

The funds had not been rebalanced or reviewed since Peter originally invested.

Some had performed acceptably and came from well-known fund groups.

Others were much more problematic.

No regular rebalancing Expensive active funds Poor-performing holdings Structured products

The previous adviser had also recommended structured products which, following volatility in global stock markets, had ended their terms with no value.

I also identified funds that had paid additional commission to the previous adviser and had subsequently performed poorly against their sectors.

Cost was part of the issue, alongside poor fund management.

Making changes

Cheaper didn't mean
simply chasing cheap.

I removed the investments I considered unsuitable and replaced many of them with lower-cost passive index-tracking funds.

The portfolio was also rebalanced to reflect Peter's current attitude to risk.

His risk tolerance had changed following his previous experience and his increased nervousness about financial markets, so the portfolio needed to reflect the person he was now — not the person who had originally invested years earlier.

Those changes reduced the portfolio's average ongoing fund management charge from approximately 1.9% a year to around 0.55%.

Planning the exit

Eventually, enough
was enough.

By the ninth year of the investment bond, Peter had decided he wanted out.

By then the surrender penalty had fallen to a more manageable 0.75% of the fund.

Waiting another year would have removed the surrender penalty altogether, but there was another important consideration.

The existing product's 1% annual charge would continue for the rest of Peter's life.

On top of that, 1% a year was being deducted from the funds for ongoing adviser servicing — despite Peter having received virtually no ongoing service from his former adviser.

The new arrangement

A different way forward.

The bond was sold and the funds reinvested into a similar product with another provider.

The new arrangement was structured so that enough commission was used to offset Peter's 0.75% surrender penalty and cover the agreed cost of my work.

0.75% surrender penalty addressed 5-year establishment period 0.45% establishment charge Lower ongoing costs

The result

Nearly 2% a year
in costs removed.

And importantly

The review wasn't
the end of the relationship.

Peter was pleased with the new arrangement and particularly valued receiving regular advice about changes to asset allocation and, when appropriate, individual funds within his portfolio.

That ongoing advice was charged at 0.5% a year — half the 1% ongoing fee previously being paid to his former advisory firm.

And there was still another area to examine: Peter's SIPP provider and the trustee fees being charged.

The bigger point

Financial arrangements shouldn't
disappear into a drawer.

Products, charges, investments, risk and your own circumstances can all change over time.

A portfolio that made sense years ago may deserve another look today.

This case study describes an individual client's circumstances at the time and is provided for general information only. It should not be taken as a recommendation that the same approach would be suitable for another person.
