A story that shaped my approach

“Just one more year’s pension.”

Jim was a teacher — a bricklayer by trade — who moved into teaching much later in life. Because of that, he hadn’t built up much by way of a pension before starting his teaching career.

He was extremely pleased to join the Teachers’ Final Salary pension scheme.

It was a stressful job. He worked at a school for what could only really be described as “naughty boys” — young people who were often one step away from prison.

But he stuck at it because he wanted to build up his pension entitlement and enjoy his future retirement with his wife.

“Just one more year’s pension.”

That was what Jim would say to his wife, year after year.

Jim wasn’t in particularly good health and was being treated with medication for high blood pressure.

Towards the end of his career he went off sick. Fortunately, the school’s employment conditions were generous, providing six months at full pay followed by six months at half pay.

Not long after turning 61, Jim retired on 1 August 1992 — which was also his wife’s birthday.

That day, they went on holiday to Devon.

They were looking forward to many more trips together during the long and happy retirement they had spent years planning.

Two days later

Jim suffered a fatal
heart attack.

The retirement he had worked towards for so long had lasted just two days.

Life doesn't
always wait.

Jim was my father.

My mother remained a widow until her own death 23 years later.

That experience is a large part of why I approach financial planning in the way I do today.

Had my father been able to see clearly what the financial effect of retiring earlier might have been, he may have made a different decision.

Five years’ less pension entitlement may have affected the numbers, but understanding the actual effect on his lifestyle could have given him another perspective.

Financial planning cannot tell us how long we have. What it can do is help us understand the choices available to us while we have them.

Money is the plan.
Life is the point.

Financial Life Planning

Knowing when you have enough.

This is what excites me about what I do: helping clients understand whether they may be able to retire and enjoy their retirement without constantly fearing that their money will run out too soon.

That can be particularly difficult for business owners. A business is often much more than an asset or source of income. It is something they have built, invested themselves in and become emotionally attached to.

Letting go can therefore be as much an emotional decision as a financial one.

Seeing the bigger picture

What if?

Using financial planning software, I can help create a picture of your current and potential future financial position.

We can then explore different “what if” scenarios and consider how changes or unexpected events might affect the plan.

It means financial planning becomes more than looking at today's pension or investment value. It becomes a way of exploring what your money may allow you to do.

What if I retire earlier? What if my plans change? What if life throws something unexpected at me? What might my future look like?

A plan that changes with you

Financial planning isn't
a one-off calculation.

Your life changes. Your finances change. Your priorities can change too.

That is why I see a financial plan as a living document — something that can be updated as your life and financial situation evolve.

I call myself a Financial Life Planner because the aim is not simply to manage money.

It is to help you plan for the retirement and future you want, understand the choices available to you and keep that plan on course when life inevitably throws something unexpected your way.

The question isn't always

“How much more can I build?”

Sometimes it's simply: “Do I already have enough?”
